## Summary

Four short chapters. Chapter V (Energy) explains how a general controls a large force through subdivision and signals, combines direct and indirect moves, stores force and releases it at the right instant, and uses deceptive appearances backed by real strength (AOW u03 §2 to §44). Chapter VI (Weak Points and Strong) is about imposing one's will: arrive first, attack where the enemy is not prepared, hide one's own dispositions so the enemy must spread out, and adapt like water (AOW u03 §49 to §117). Chapter VII (Manoeuvring) weighs speed against supply, gives figures on the losses of forced marches, quotes an older manual on signals, and lists rules on morale and on when not to fight (AOW u03 §121 to §206). Chapter VIII (Variation of Tactics) says a general must know when not to apply the usual rules, must weigh advantage and harm together, and must avoid five character faults (AOW u03 §211 to §255).

For students of strategy, deception or leadership. Limits: a translation of a partly corrupt original (AOW u03 §133); Giles writes as a Briton of 1910, with dated and sometimes prejudiced remarks (AOW u03 §236); most examples are commentators' anecdotes written centuries after Sun Tzu.

## Key concepts and definitions

- **Subdivision (dividing up their numbers)**: Sun Tzu says a large army is controlled like a small group once it is split into units under their own officers; fighting with many is then a matter of signs and signals (AOW u03 §2, §4). Giles glosses the units as regiments and companies (AOW u03 §3).
- **Direct and indirect (cheng and ch'i)**: Sun Tzu: the direct method engages, the indirect method wins; the two are only two "methods", yet their combinations never run out, like five notes, five colours or five tastes (AOW u03 §8, §12 to §16). Commentators disagree: Li Ch'uan, cheng faces the enemy and ch'i is a side move; Mei Yao-ch'en, ch'i is active and cheng passive; Ho Shih and a comment on Emperor T'ai Tsung, each turns into the other depending on what the enemy expects (AOW u03 §6). Giles's own summary: cheng is any move the enemy is watching, ch'i is the one that surprises him, and a surprise that is spotted turns into cheng (AOW u03 §6).
- **Energy (momentum) and decision (timing)**: Sun Tzu compares energy to a bent crossbow and decision to releasing the trigger; the good fighter is terrible in the onset and short in his decision (AOW u03 §20, §22). Giles reads "decision" as the restraint to hold back until the right distance, then strike (AOW u03 §19, §21), and says no commentator grasped the crossbow image (AOW u03 §23).
- **Simulated disorder, fear and weakness**: Sun Tzu: each feint requires the real opposite quality, that is discipline, courage and strength (AOW u03 §26); order under apparent disorder rests on subdivision, hidden courage on stored energy, hidden strength on dispositions (AOW u03 §28 to §32). Tu Mu spells out the logic (AOW u03 §27).
- **Combined energy (shih)**: Sun Tzu: rely on the force of the whole army, not on individuals, like round stones rolling down a steep slope (AOW u03 §39, §42, §44). Ts'ao Kung calls it the use of natural power (AOW u03 §43).
- **Weak points and strong (hsü and shih)**: Sun Tzu: arrive first and fresh, move the enemy at will by baits or threats, attack only undefended places and hold only places that cannot be attacked (AOW u03 §49 to §62). Wang Hsi lists what makes a place weak: a poor general, low morale, weak walls, loose precautions, late relief, short supplies, internal quarrels (AOW u03 §61).
- **Concentration against division**: Sun Tzu: if we see the enemy and stay unseen, we stay united while he must split to guard every point; then we are "many to his few" (AOW u03 §74 to §83).
- **Formlessness (water)**: Sun Tzu: tactics flow like water away from heights; war has no constant conditions (AOW u03 §107 to §111).
- **Speed and secrecy (wind, forest, fire, mountain)**: Sun Tzu: practise dissimulation and move only for a real gain; be fast as wind, dense as forest, fierce as fire when raiding, still as a mountain; keep plans dark as night and strike like a thunderbolt (AOW u03 §149, §151, §153 to §161). Mei Yao-ch'en: wind also leaves no tracks (AOW u03 §154). Giles: Turenne stressed deceiving the enemy about one's numbers (AOW u03 §150).
- **Robbing spirit and presence of mind**: Sun Tzu: an army can lose its spirit and its general his composure (AOW u03 §185, §187). Li Ching, quoted by Giles: attack must also target the enemy's mental balance (AOW u03 §188).
- **Artifice of deviation**: Sun Tzu: take the long way round, lure the enemy aside, and still arrive first; turning the devious into the direct is the hardest part of manoeuvring (AOW u03 §126, §128, §169).
- **Nine Variations**: the literal title of chapter VIII. Giles follows Wang Hsi: "nine" just means "very many"; Chang Yu links them to the Nine Situations of chapter XI; Giles also suspects lost text (AOW u03 §210).
- **Five Advantages**: named by Sun Tzu without detail (AOW u03 §227); Chia Lin lists them as the short road, the isolated army, the weak town, the position that can be stormed, and the ruler's orders (AOW u03 §228).

## Processes and checklists

1. **Organise before fighting.** Sun Tzu: subdivide the army and fix signals so that apparent chaos in battle is not real disorder (AOW u03 §2, §4, §24); Mei Yao-ch'en adds that the pre-set subdivisions are what make the scattering and regrouping safe (AOW u03 §25). Harmonise the army before camping (AOW u03 §122); Chang Yu: build trust between ranks first (AOW u03 §123).
2. **Engage directly, win indirectly.** Sun Tzu: fix the enemy with the direct move, decide with the indirect one, and keep switching roles (AOW u03 §8, §16). Chang Yu: hit the flanks or the rear (AOW u03 §9).
3. **Store energy, then release it at the right moment** (AOW u03 §22). Giles: hold fire until the range is effective (AOW u03 §19).
4. **Control the enemy's movements with appearances.** Sun Tzu: show deceptive signs, offer a bait, and wait with picked troops (AOW u03 §34, §36, §37). Tu Mu: look weak if stronger, look strong if weaker (AOW u03 §35).
5. **Force or refuse battle at will.** Sun Tzu: to fight a sheltered enemy, attack a place he must relieve (AOW u03 §70); to avoid battle, put something strange in his way (AOW u03 §72). Tu Mu: cut his roads home, or threaten his ruler (AOW u03 §71).
6. **Probe the enemy.** Sun Tzu: compare plans, provoke him to learn his intentions, force him to show his weak spots, compare forces point by point (AOW u03 §91, §93, §95, §96). Chang Yu: read his joy or anger (AOW u03 §94).
7. **Hide your own dispositions** so that no spy or clever officer can plan against you (AOW u03 §98, §100); do not repeat a winning method (AOW u03 §105).
8. **Weigh the march.** Sun Tzu: a full army is too slow, a light column loses its stores (AOW u03 §132); know allies' designs and the terrain, use local guides (AOW u03 §145 to §147).
9. **Read morale.** Sun Tzu: avoid the enemy when his spirit is keen, attack when it flags; wait calm for his disorder; be near, rested and fed while he is far, tired and hungry (AOW u03 §192 to §194).
10. **Vary the rules.** Sun Tzu: some roads, armies, towns, positions and even orders of the sovereign must be refused (AOW u03 §216 to §222); blend advantage and disadvantage in every plan (AOW u03 §229, §231, §233); wear down hostile leaders, keep them busy and lure them with false attractions (AOW u03 §235, §239, §241); rely on your own readiness, not on the enemy staying away (AOW u03 §243).

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Defending everywhere**: Sun Tzu: reinforcing every point makes you weak at every point (AOW u03 §81). Giles quotes Frederick the Great on inexperienced generals who guard everything (AOW u03 §82).
- **Long forced marches for a tactical gain**: the losses of the table above (AOW u03 §137, §139); Ts'ao Kung's moral: do not march 100 li for an advantage (AOW u03 §138).
- **Taking the bait**: Sun Tzu: do not pursue a feigned flight or swallow a bait (AOW u03 §197, §198). Li Ch'uan and Tu Mu read the bait as poisoned food; Giles mocks this, and Ch'en Hao and Chang Yu give the wider sense (AOW u03 §199).
- **Pressing a desperate enemy**: Sun Tzu: leave an outlet, do not push a cornered foe (AOW u03 §202, §204). Tu Mu: the outlet is a trick to stop him fighting with despair, not mercy (AOW u03 §203).
- **Five faults of a general** (Sun Tzu): recklessness, cowardice, hasty temper, oversensitive honour, over-solicitude for the men; one of them is always behind a lost army and a slain leader (AOW u03 §244 to §255). Giles insists that honour and care for troops are not faults in themselves, only their excess (AOW u03 §251, §253).

## Examples and studies cited
*(studies, cases and anecdotes; for understanding only, never reused as is. No study in the modern sense here: only historical cases and anecdotes, mostly told by commentators)*

- **Han Hsin's feint at Lin-chin** (Ho Shih, citing the Ch'ien Han Shu): open march as cheng, river crossing in wooden tubs as ch'i (AOW u03 §6).
- **Kao Tsu at Po-teng** (Chang Yu): the Hsiung-nu showed only weak men and beasts, the spies urged attack, the emperor was surrounded (AOW u03 §33).
- **Sun Pin's shrinking fires, 341 B.C.** (Tu Mu): fires reduced night after night to fake desertion, then an ambush at a defile. Giles notes that the Shih Chi tells a less dramatic end (AOW u03 §35).
- **Chu-ko Liang's open gates** (Tu Mu): an empty-looking city made Ssu-ma I fear an ambush and retreat; Giles calls it "bluff" (AOW u03 §73).
- **Chao She's march, 270 B.C.** (Tu Mu): fake delay reported by spies, then a sudden march to seize a hill (AOW u03 §129).
- **Wu Ch'i executes a brave soldier** who fought without orders (Tu Mu) (AOW u03 §181).
- **Ts'ao Kuei waits for the third drum roll** (Li Ch'uan and others, from the Tso Chuan) (AOW u03 §186).
- **Other commentator anecdotes**: Ts'ao Ts'ao's tunnel ambush, 198 A.D. (AOW u03 §201); Fu Yen-ch'ing's sandstorm charge, 945 A.D. (AOW u03 §205); Huan Hsuan's escape boat, 404 A.D. (AOW u03 §247); Yao Hsiang provoked into battle, 357 A.D. (AOW u03 §249).
- **Giles's Western parallels** (AOW u03 §9, §19, §79, §84, §86, §127, §138, §150, §190, §221, §253): Roberts, Nelson, Grant, Henderson, Blücher, Hannibal, Napoleon, Stonewall Jackson, Turenne, Ladysmith.
